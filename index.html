What the Data Show

The first analysis in this series asks how far state literacy laws reach multilingual learners. It finds a mixed but improving landscape — more states are referencing multilingual learners, but inclusion and integration are not the same thing.

1 37 states now reference multilingual learners in their literacy legislation — more than double the 16 states in 2019. Growth came in two diffusion waves (2021 and 2023) after the initial science-of-reading surge, followed by consolidation in 2024–2025.
2 The number of states whose literacy legislation reaches at least one instructional-core domain has risen from 9 in 2019 to 23 in 2025. Cumulative depth has nearly doubled as well — the average state's literacy legislation now addresses 1.68 of the 5 core domains, up from 0.88 in 2019.
3 Only 8 of the 37 reach the broadest classification — multilingual-learner provisions embedded across five or more domains with at least a modest presence in the instructional core. A further 15 reach the core more thinly, and 14 stay entirely outside it. Even within the broadest group, depth varies: some states earn the classification by accumulating thin provisions across several laws rather than through one integrated statute.
4 The most consistent pattern in the dataset is siloing: EL provisions and science-of-reading provisions are developed in separate legislative vehicles, producing bills that are individually defensible but collectively incoherent. Nevada and Washington illustrate this most clearly.
5 California, Connecticut, Georgia, Illinois, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada and Texas are the eight states where multilingual-learner and reading-research provisions are most consistently held together in statute — demonstrating that integrated legislation is achievable, while leaving room to grow.
Methodology

About This Analysis

These analyses draw on the ASI Legislation Database, maintained by the Albert Shanker Institute. It holds every literacy law enacted in the 50 states and the District of Columbia; 543 fall inside the 2019–2025 window and 518 are in scope once laws that touch literacy only administratively are set aside. The database records enacted law only — no bill that failed, died in committee or is still pending appears here. Multilingual-learner provisions are hand-coded by domain using a nine-category framework.

Classification Framework

Jurisdictions are classified along two dimensions: scope (how many of the nine multilingual-learner domains the laws touch) and location (instructional core or periphery). Broadest means five or more domains with at least a modest presence in the core. Instructional-core domains are instructional and programme design, teacher professional development, teacher preparation, assessment and screening, and targeted resources and staffing.

Law-Level Indicators

Each law carries several indicators: whether it is the jurisdiction’s flagship literacy law, whether it names the five pillars of reading, oral language and background knowledge, and whether it names the literacy research base at all — the science of reading, evidence-based reading, a pillar of reading, or dyslexia. Together these show how far a law is grounded in the reading research.

Integration vs. Siloing

A provision counts as integrated when it appears in a law that also covers the five pillars and oral language. Siloed provisions sit in laws outside that framework. Where both exist for the same domain within a jurisdiction, both are shown if they address different aspects.

Study Period

The window is 2019–2025, coinciding with the surge in reading-research legislation. Predecessor flagship laws enacted before 2019 (Mississippi 2013, Utah 2018) sit outside it, so a jurisdiction whose framework predates the window may read thinner here than it is.
